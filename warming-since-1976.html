Why global temperature has risen since 1976 — and what made the 1940s bump

A detailed look at each driver behind the curve on the Planet tab: how much it contributed, when, and how sure scientists are.

The general statement. Since 1976 the world has warmed by about 1.0 °C, at roughly five times the rate of 1880–1975. The long-term rise is caused by greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels; that is settled. The timing of the turn in the mid-1970s came from two other things happening at once: air-pollution haze, which had been masking greenhouse warming since the 1940s, stopped growing in Europe and North America; and the Pacific flipped into a warm phase in 1976–77. On top of the steady climb, El Niño years make spikes, and big volcanic eruptions make dips. The Sun did not drive it. Since about 2010 warming has run faster again. Sharp cuts in sulphur pollution from China and from shipping removed more of the mask, and the planet now reflects less sunlight because some low clouds have thinned; how big the speed-up is, and how much each cause contributed, are still debated.

Who did what is broken down in section 9: each activity, gas and country, the events behind them, and what would reduce each part.

Measuring from 1976 alone overstates the rise: 1976 (−0.10 °C) came at the end of a long 1973–76 La Niña, a cold phase of the Pacific. On the IPCC's 1850–1900 baseline, the latest international assessment puts the 2015–2024 decade at 1.24 °C above pre-industrial and 2024 alone at about 1.52 °C, of which an estimated 1.36 °C was human-induced.1

2. Greenhouse gases — the main driver

  • CO₂ in the air: 332 ppm in 1976 and 427 ppm in 2025 at Mauna Loa (annual means, NOAA). That is a 29% rise in 49 years.
  • Emissions behind it: fossil CO₂ emissions more than doubled, from 17.9 billion tonnes in 1976 to 38.6 billion tonnes in 2024. Coal rose from 6.2 to 15.8 billion tonnes, a record, oil from 8.5 to 12.5 and gas from 2.4 to 8.0. Deforestation and other land-use change added another 4.6 billion tonnes in 2024 (Global Carbon Budget 2025).20
  • Total heat-trapping effect: NOAA's Annual Greenhouse Gas Index rose 54% between 1990 and 2024. It took about 240 years to reach the 1990 level and only 34 more years to add another 54%. CO₂ contributes most, then methane.3
  • CFCs — an extra push in the 1970s and 1980s: the ozone-destroying gases are also strong greenhouse gases. Their warming effect grew fast from 1950 to about 1990, then the Montreal Protocol (1987) stopped the growth; CFC-11 has fallen since the mid-1990s.4 So these gases added to the steep climb of 1975–1990, and their contribution has levelled off since.
  • How much warming: the IPCC assesses that well-mixed greenhouse gases likely caused 1.0–2.0 °C of warming from 1850–1900 to 2010–2019 — more than the total observed, because aerosols cancelled part of it.5

Fingerprint: the stratosphere has cooled while the surface warmed. A brighter Sun would warm both; heat-trapping gases near the surface produce exactly this split.6

3. Aerosols — the mask that slipped

Sulphur dioxide from coal and oil forms tiny sulphate particles that reflect sunlight and brighten clouds. They cool the planet, and they fall out of the air within days, so their effect tracks current emissions.

  • 1940s–1970s: rapidly rising pollution after the war largely offset greenhouse warming — the flat middle of the chart.
  • The turn: sulphur emissions peaked around 1970 in North America and around 1980 in Europe, after clean-air laws; the global total peaked in the 1980s.7 Once the mask stopped thickening, greenhouse warming showed through.
  • China: emissions peaked in 2006; East Asia then cut sulphur dioxide by about 75% in roughly 15 years. A 2025 study (Samset and co-authors, Communications Earth & Environment) concludes this cleanup has likely driven much of the recent speed-up in warming.8
  • Shipping, 2020: the International Maritime Organization cut the sulphur allowed in ship fuel. A 2024 study led by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory found the expected loss of cooling over busy shipping lanes matches observed cloud changes and contributed to 2022–2023 Northern Hemisphere heat, but is too small to be the only cause; the authors call their estimate possibly conservative.9
  • In the emissions data: global sulphur dioxide peaked in 1979 at 138 million tonnes and fell to 69 million tonnes by 2023. Power plants alone peaked later, in 1990, at 59 million tonnes. Ship emissions fell from 10.4 million tonnes in 2019 to 2.0 million tonnes in 2020, an 81% cut in one year (CEDS).22
  • How much cooling in total: the IPCC puts the cooling from aerosols and other non-greenhouse human factors at 0.0–0.8 °C by 2010–2019.5 That wide range is the largest single uncertainty in explaining the past century — and the reason the size of the recent acceleration is disputed.

4. The Pacific's 1976–77 shift — the timing

In 1976–77 the North Pacific changed regime: the Pacific Decadal Oscillation switched from its cool to its warm phase, and the Aleutian Low strengthened for about three decades (1977–2005), after a weak period from 1947 to 1976. It altered how El Niño evolves and how it affects weather across the Americas, Asia and Australia.10 Earlier shifts of the same kind happened in 1924–25 and 1946–47.

The PDO does not add heat to the planet; it moves it between the ocean and the air. So it explains why the climb started so sharply around 1976–77, not why it kept going for fifty years. The IPCC notes the PDO appears to respond to the same forcing as these shifts rather than trigger them.10

5. Natural ups and downs on top of the trend

El Niño and La Niña

Strong El Niños lift global temperature for about a year, usually peaking a few months after the Pacific does. The big ones in the record since 1976, by peak ONI (ERSSTv6, 3-month means): 1982–83 +2.14, 1997–98 +2.37, 2015–16 +2.59, 2023–24 +1.99 — and now 2026, already +2.16 for July–September. Each lifted global temperature in the year that followed (1983 less than expected, because El Chichón's haze offset it); La Niña years give the dips.

Volcanoes

  • El Chichón, Mexico, 1982 and Pinatubo, Philippines, 1991 put sulphur high into the stratosphere. Pinatubo injected about 15 million tonnes of sulphur dioxide and cooled the globe by about 0.6 °C for around 15 months; the effect lasted almost two years.11 These eruptions explain the dips of 1982–84 and 1992–93.
  • Hunga Tonga, 2022 added a huge amount of water vapour, a greenhouse gas, as well as sulphate. A NASA-led study found its net effect was a slight cooling, so it did not cause the 2023–24 heat.12

The Sun

Solar output follows its 11-year cycle with no net increase since the 1950s, and satellites since 1978 show no upward trend.6 The recent solar maximum (cycle 25) adds a small, temporary push; it cannot explain a 50-year rise.

6. The 1998–2012 slowdown

Surface warming slowed for about a decade after the 1998 El Niño. The heat did not disappear: Pacific trade winds strengthened from the 1990s and pushed extra heat into the subsurface western Pacific (England and co-authors, Nature Climate Change, 2014). The authors expected it to return once the winds eased.13 It did: 2014–2016 brought three consecutive records.

7. 2023–2026: a jump, and an acceleration debate

  • The planet is absorbing more energy: satellite and ocean data show Earth's energy imbalance roughly doubled between 2005 and 2019 — from fewer reflective clouds and less sea ice, more greenhouse gases and water vapour, and the PDO's swing to a warm phase (Loeb and co-authors, Geophysical Research Letters, 2021).14
  • The 2023 gap: greenhouse gases, El Niño and volcanoes together leave about 0.2 °C of the 2023 record unexplained. A Science study led by the Alfred Wegener Institute attributes most of it to a decline in certain low clouds, which made the planet less reflective.15 Whether that cloud loss is a response to cleaner air, natural variability or a warming feedback is still open.
  • How fast now? The international indicators team puts the human-induced rate at 0.27 °C per decade for 2015–2024, unprecedented in the instrumental record, partly due to weaker aerosol cooling.1 Foster and Rahmstorf (Geophysical Research Letters, 2026) remove El Niño, volcanic and solar effects and find about 0.2 °C per decade for 1970–2015 and about 0.35 °C per decade since 2015.16 Others disagree on the size: Michael Mann sees no acceleration beyond the aerosol effect; Zeke Hausfather prefers about 0.27 °C; Piers Forster prefers "unprecedented rates" to "acceleration".16 Over 1970–2008 the rate was about 0.18 °C per decade, so warming is now faster on every estimate; the debate is about by how much.17

8. The warm bump around the Second World War

The early-1940s spike on the chart is partly real and partly a measurement artefact:

  • Real: a strong, long El Niño ran from 1940 to 1942. Brönnimann and co-authors (Nature, 2004) reconstructed its global effects, including a cold Europe and a warm Alaska, and described it as a recurring extreme state of the global climate system linked to strong El Niños.18
  • Artefact: most ocean temperatures came from ships. Before the war many fleets measured sea water in canvas buckets, which cool by evaporation and read low — about 0.4 °C cooler on average than modern methods. From about 1939–41 the record came almost entirely from US ships, which read engine-intake water and run warm. After 1945 British, Dutch and Japanese bucket readings returned, producing an artificial drop. Thompson and co-authors (Nature, 2008) identified the 1945 discontinuity. Modern ocean datasets, including the ERSST data inside GISTEMP, now apply bias corrections for these changes in method, but the wartime years remain among the least certain in the record.19

So the 1940s peak is not evidence of a warming episode like today's; it is an El Niño seen partly through biased instruments. The later 1950s–70s plateau is real and reflects rising aerosol pollution.

9. Who did what

A statement such as "people warmed the planet by 1 °C" is true but tells you nothing you can act on. This section works like a household energy log: what happened, who did it, how much each part added, and what would reduce it. It follows one chain:

9.2 What pushed the climate since the 1970s

Between the 1971–80 and 2015–24 decade averages, the human push on the climate grew by 2.20 W/m². Spread over the whole Earth, that is about 1,100 trillion watts of extra energy kept in the system, continuously.21

  • Carbon dioxide: +1.20 W/m², 55% of the increase. The single biggest item.
  • Less aerosol haze: +0.27 W/m², 12%. Not a new emission but a lost shield: cleaner air let more sunlight through (section 3).
  • CFCs, HCFCs and HFCs: +0.26 W/m², 12%. Refrigerants, aerosol-can propellants and foams. Most of it came before 1990; the Montreal Protocol then stopped the growth of the ozone-depleting kinds.
  • Methane: +0.15 W/m², 7%, and low-level ozone (smog): +0.14 W/m², 7%. Ozone forms in the lower atmosphere from methane, traffic and power-plant nitrogen oxides, and carbon monoxide.
  • Nitrous oxide: +0.11 W/m², 5%, mainly from farming. Aircraft contrails: +0.04 W/m², 2%.
  • Natural factors were slightly negative: the Sun −0.03 and volcanoes −0.02 W/m² between the same decades.

9.3 How the push became warming

Jones and co-authors convert each country's emissions of the three main greenhouse gases into warming, year by year. On their numbers, these three gases had caused 0.75 °C of warming by 1976 and 1.68 °C by 2024: +0.93 °C in 48 years. Carbon dioxide added 0.71 °C of that, methane 0.17 °C and nitrous oxide 0.05 °C.27

This split covers only those three gases; it leaves out the CFC, ozone and aerosol effects in 9.2, which have no country breakdown. It is the standard published way to answer "whose emissions caused how much".

9.4 Which activities

Carbon dioxide, 1976–2024: 1,636 billion tonnes in total. Coal 525 billion tonnes (32%), oil 500 (31%), deforestation and other land-use change 295 (18%), natural gas 242 (15%), cement 44 (3%), gas flaring 16 (1%) and other industry 14 (1%).20

Methane from human sources rose from 260 to 347 million tonnes a year between 1976 and 2023 (+33%).22

  • Livestock (digestion and manure): 88 → 116 million tonnes, the largest source.
  • Landfills and wastewater: 35 → 65 million tonnes, nearly double.
  • Coal mining: 25 → 52 million tonnes, doubled with coal output.
  • Oil and gas: 82 → 75 million tonnes in this inventory. The IEA's satellite-informed estimate is higher, 81 million tonnes from oil and gas in 2025 alone, so treat this line as a lower bound.24
  • Rice paddies: 22 → 25 million tonnes.

Natural sources such as wetlands are not included; they are not human activities, although warming may be increasing them.

9.5 Which countries

Of the 0.93 °C added by the three gases since 1976:27

  • China: 0.171 °C (18%). Fossil CO₂ nearly tripled in 13 years, from 3.6 billion tonnes in 2000 to 9.9 billion in 2013, mostly coal.
  • United States: 0.126 °C (14%). Emissions per person are three times the world average: 14.2 tonnes of CO₂ in 2024, against 4.7.
  • Brazil: 0.060 °C (6.5%). Mostly deforestation: 77 of its 94 billion tonnes of CO₂ since 1976 came from land-use change.
  • Russia: 0.052 °C (5.6%).
  • India: 0.050 °C (5.4%). Fossil CO₂ grew 13-fold, from 0.24 billion tonnes in 1976 to 3.2 billion in 2024, but is still 2.2 tonnes per person.
  • Indonesia: 0.036 °C (3.9%). Mostly land-use change: 42 of its 58 billion tonnes of CO₂.
  • Places 7–22: Japan 0.024 °C; Canada 0.018; Germany 0.017; Mexico 0.014; Australia, Saudi Arabia, South Africa and Iran 0.012 each; South Korea and Ukraine 0.010; Italy, Nigeria, Pakistan, Thailand, Turkey and the United Kingdom 0.009 each.
  • The 193 other countries together: 0.238 °C (26%).

By income group: high-income countries (1.4 billion people) added 0.36 °C and upper-middle-income countries, including China (2.9 billion), 0.39 °C. Lower-middle-income countries (3.1 billion) added 0.13 °C and low-income countries (0.8 billion) 0.04 °C.27
