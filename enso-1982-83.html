Scope, index and data

This page holds the evidence assembled so far (stages 1–3 of 5). The paper's prose will be drafted from it after review. Primary index: NOAA's Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI), official since 1 February 2026 [18] [14]: the three-month mean of Niño 3.4 minus the 20°N–20°S tropical-mean anomaly, rescaled. On RONI the 1982–83 peak is +2.40 (DJF 1983), the strongest of the three historical events in this series; on the traditional ONI it is +2.14.

Observing system in 1982. No TAO mooring array (completed in 1994), no satellite altimetry (from 1992) and no Argo floats. The evidence rests on ship and XBT tracks, island tide gauges, a few moorings (e.g. 0°, 110°W) and later reanalyses. Satellite SST from April 1982 was biased low by up to 2.5 °C by El Chichón aerosol [1]. Subsurface indices for 1981–82 are reconstructions from sparse data.

MJO contribution (assessment)

Answer so far. Multiple diagnostics and daily-field inspection support identifying the December 1981 and late March 1982 episodes as MJO-like intraseasonal activity, the late April–May 1982 sequence as MJO-like in OLR-based measures, and late June 1982 as probable MJO-like activity. One peer-reviewed reconstruction links the wind forcing to an ocean response [55]. In it, an MJO in May–June 1982 favoured a westerly burst and a downwelling Kelvin wave in June–July, which the authors suggest may have been largely responsible for the rapid development of El Niño conditions in July. Christmas Island sea level rose about 25 cm as that wave passed and stayed high through December. The same study finds the documented May burst produced only a low-amplitude Kelvin wave. On that basis the MJO is a probable contributor to growth in June–July 1982 (grade B: one event-specific reconstruction with stated biases, no model experiment isolating the effect). Its role in initiation remains unresolved. From late 1982 the MJO was itself altered by El Niño, so the interaction ran both ways.

Event-specific literature.

  • Weickmann (1991), abstract only: six MJOs in September 1981–April 1982, with interannual transitions coinciding with strong MJO passages into the Pacific [27].
  • Li et al. (2023), full text: an ERA-Interim and BoM RMM analysis of the three super El Niños [53]. It finds eastward-propagating MJO activity in the developing stage (February–September) of all three, but the weakest eastward power in 1982/83 (main period about 40 days). It reports two prominent eastward propagations of MJO westerlies in that stage. In their Figure 2A, RMM exceeds 1.2 in early April and late May–early June 1982. The authors note that the MJO's role in 1982 "had not been deeply examined due to the lack of data". They link the weak, eastward-shifted mature-stage MJO to El Niño-driven circulation and moisture anomalies, and warn that RMM and zonal-wind amplitude can give contradictory results.
  • Roundy and Kiladis (2007), full text [55]. Equatorial dynamic height back to 1974 is reconstructed by regressing TAO data on daily sea level at 11 island and coastal stations plus an eastern-Pacific OLR index. The highest-amplitude Kelvin waves of 1982 were in December 1981–January 1982, March–April, June–July and November–December. The December 1981–January 1982 wave coincided with the first shift of the winds toward westerly anomalies. The June–July wave was directly preceded by MJO convection moving from the Indian Ocean into the western Pacific; Christmas Island sea level jumped about 25 cm with its passage in July and stayed high through December. The May burst produced a low-amplitude wave, and the November wave was the most intense but came after El Niño had peaked. The authors caution that the reconstruction underestimates amplitudes and that the 1982 winds contain substantial biases. Their conference abstract makes the same points [54]; their 2006 paper covers 1988–2005 only [56].
  • Chu and Frederick (1990) document the May 1982 burst and attribute its origin to midlatitude pressure forcing; they do not discuss the MJO [28].

The indices are not independent: OMI, FMO, RMM and RMM* all use NOAA interpolated OLR; RMM, RMM* and VPM use NCEP winds; and our diagnostics below reuse the same interpolated OLR and NCEP/NCAR R1 winds. Agreement between them is useful but is not independent validation. VPM phase numbers are not used because its sign convention was not verified.

Our three diagnostics (documented; not validated MJO indices). Code: tools/papers/mjo_1982_diag.py; outputs in data/papers/1982/.

  • Data. NCEP/NCAR Reanalysis 1 daily u at 850, 200 and 1000 hPa (2.5° grid) and NOAA interpolated daily OLR, 1979–2001, via PSL THREDDS [39] [61].
  • Averaging. Cosine-weighted mean over 10°S–10°N (1000 hPa: 5°S–5°N), giving one value per 2.5° of longitude.
  • Anomalies. Departures from a 1979–2000 climatology: the mean plus the first three annual harmonics, fitted by least squares.
  • D1 (Wheeler–Hendon style). Subtract the mean of the previous 120 days. Scale each field by its standard deviation over all longitudes and days. Run a combined EOF (SVD) of OLR, u850 and u200 over 1979–2001. Scale the first two principal components to unit variance, and set the sign of PC2 so that PC1 leads it at a 10-day lag. Amplitude = √(PC1² + PC2²). No ENSO regression is removed, unlike BoM RMM. The two EOFs explain 13.0% and 11.2% of variance.
  • D2 (band-pass). As D1, but the anomalies are FFT band-passed to 20–100 days instead of removing the 120-day mean (21.1% and 19.8%).
  • D3 (OLR only). EOFs of band-passed OLR alone (24.6% and 17.1%).
  • Bursts. Unfiltered 1000 hPa anomaly ≥ 5 m s⁻¹ for ≥ 3 days over ≥ 10° of longitude within 120°E–150°W.

Propagation test. Code: tools/papers/mjo_1982_tests.py. All four parts use the 20–100-day fields at 10°S–10°N:

  1. T1. Indian Ocean convection: OLR ≤ −15 W m⁻² somewhere in 70°–100°E.
  2. T2. Western-Pacific convection: OLR ≤ −15 W m⁻² in 140°E–180°, 8–35 days after T1. Over the ~65° between the boxes this means 2.4–10.4 m s⁻¹, bracketing the typical ~5 m s⁻¹ [57].
  3. T3. Lag-correlation speed: correlate OLR at 60°E–180° with the 70°–100°E mean at lags of −5 to +35 days. Fitting the best lag at each longitude (r ≥ 0.4) must give 2–10 m s⁻¹ eastward.
  4. T4. Circulation: within ±2 days of the western-Pacific convection, u850 over the 30° west of its centre is westerly and u200 easterly on at least 3 of 5 days.

A first version that tracked the single daily OLR minimum failed every window, because the minimum jumps between systems and convection weakens over the Maritime Continent; it was replaced before results were interpreted.

Base rates. In sliding 45-day windows from January 1981 to May 1983, T1 + T2 + T4 hold in 55% of windows and all four tests in 19%. Passing T1, T2 and T4 alone is therefore weak evidence; we treat all four as the strict test.

What this supports. Multiple diagnostics and the daily fields support identifying the December 1981 and late March 1982 episodes, and the late April–May 1982 sequence in OLR-based measures, as MJO-like intraseasonal activity. Our MJO-like windows (December 1981, late March, late June and November 1982) match the months of the strongest reconstructed Kelvin waves [55]. For June–July 1982 that study supplies the missing link from MJO wind forcing to an ocean response, so a contribution to growth is probable. It remains one reconstruction, with biased winds and no model run isolating the effect, so it is not shown. Ocean-model studies show that MJO winds can force Kelvin waves and rectify into low-frequency SST anomalies [59] [60], and Kelvin-wave forcing by the MJO preceded larger eastern-Pacific SST anomalies in 1980–99 warm events [35]; none of these isolates the effect in 1982.

Two-way interaction. From October 1982 MJO convection reached 170°–155°W, and October–March intraseasonal OLR variance over 60°–150°E was the second lowest of 22 seasons (1979–2001; only 1997–98 was lower). This matches the eastward shift and reduced activity reported for exceptional warm events [33] [34], and Li et al.'s finding that El Niño-driven circulation and moisture changes altered the mature-stage MJO in 1982/83 [53]. These features are a consequence of El Niño, not evidence that the MJO drove it.

Bursts and base rate. By the burst definition above, 1982 had 13 bursts against a 1979–2001 mean of 4.4 a year (range 0–16; 1997 had 16). Part of the late-1982 count reflects the El Niño's own slow westerly anomaly. Most bursts in 1979–2002 were tied to MJO-like convection, but MJO amplitude itself was not correlated with burst frequency [36].

Next steps. Test the windows with a second reanalysis (JRA-55 or ERA5); compare Christmas Island and other island sea levels directly; and look for an ocean-model hindcast of 1982 forced with and without intraseasonal winds (Bergman et al. 2001, cited by Roundy and Kiladis, is a candidate).

That supports the general mechanism; it does not document the 1982 episodes. The May 1982 study cited in the Vecchi–Harrison atlas is Chu and Frederick (1990) [30] [28]. El Chichón as an external forcing (assessment)

Initiation and subsequent growth are judged separately.

Initiation: not supported by the timing; formally unresolved. The eruptions were on 29 March and 3–4 April 1982 [45]. Before them the equatorial Pacific was already recharged — warm water volume +2.15 × 10¹⁴ m³ in March, the 96th percentile of 1980–2025 months — and western-Pacific trades were already weaker than normal (Feb–Apr), after the strong December 1981 MJO [62] [27]. Khodri et al. note that ocean reanalyses show high equatorial heat content before El Chichón, and that a warm event was already under way during two of the four large historical eruptions they list (Santa María 1902, Agung 1963, El Chichón 1982, Pinatubo 1991), without naming which two [13]. In GFDL CM2.1 ensembles the ENSO response depends on the ocean state and timing, and when a strong El Niño is already developing at an April eruption the volcanic effect is unlikely to be visible [42]. With only three observed cases, Chai et al. state that the westerlies after Agung, El Chichón and Pinatubo cannot be attributed to the eruptions rather than internal variability [43]. Robock et al. (1995) are reported by later papers to have concluded that the 1982 event began before the eruption; that paper was not read, so this is indirect evidence [46].

Growth: possible weak amplifier, unresolved. Several models produce westerly anomalies over the western-central Pacific within months of a tropical eruption — through tropical African cooling and a Kelvin-wave response [13], through South–Southeast Asian and West African drying (8 of 11 models) [43], or through Maritime Continent cooling [44]. Kim et al. propose that faster land drying around the warm pool helps the MJO propagate into the western Pacific, raising burst frequency (by about 76% in CESM2) and the chance of El Niño onset [41]. For 1982 that pathway bears on growth rather than onset: their observed signals are in July–November 1982, after the documented May burst and the June westerlies; Maritime Continent drying and enhanced central-Pacific MJO activity are also normal El Niño responses; the ensemble was screened on an April–June recharge index that includes post-eruption months (0.9σ in 1982, which the authors class as neutral); and HadGEM3 did not reproduce the result. Proxy records show no consistent ENSO response to eruptions over the last millennium [4].

Observing-system effect (certain). Volcanic aerosol biased satellite SST low by up to 2.5 °C [1]. A reprocessed OSTIA record found unexplained biases in May and Oct–Dec 1982 in its single AVHRR input, which the authors attribute mainly to instrument calibration rather than the eruption [45]. RONI and ONI use the in-situ ERSST record.

Statistics (base rates and lead–lag). DMI and Niño 3.4 in SON correlate at r = 0.59 (n = 75). Regressing SON DMI on SON Niño 3.4 (excluding 1982) predicts +0.61 °C for 1982; the observed value was +0.58 °C (residual 0.07 standard deviations). By this measure the 1982 IOD was as large as ENSO alone would make it. The JJA DMI adds no information about NDJ Niño 3.4 once JJA Niño 3.4 is known (partial r = 0.02). The SON DMI is negatively related to the following year's NDJ Niño 3.4 after removing SON Niño 3.4 (partial r = −0.30), in line with the reported tendency for a positive IOD to be followed by La Niña [52]. Of 17 years with SON DMI above +0.4 °C (1950–2024), 11 coincided with El Niño.

Initiation. No evidence that the Indian Ocean initiated the event. The early eastern-pole cooling preceded Pacific warming but lacked the wind feedback, and no study tests it for 1982.

Growth and peak. Pacific forcing of the IOD is well supported: the El Niño-related loss of Maritime Continent convection drives easterlies over the eastern Indian Ocean [50]. Feedback the other way is supported only by models that include 1982: forecasts with Indian Ocean coupling suppressed underestimate the 1982–83 peak, though that model over-predicted the 1982 IOD [47]; a simple model driven by IOD-induced winds (heating taken from the 1982 and 1997 OLR composites) reproduces eastern-Pacific intensification [48]; decoupling the Indian and Atlantic Oceans weakens super El Niños, with coupling between the two oceans the key factor [49]. Against this, one coupled model found no significant Pacific impact from a fall IOD [51], and a pIOD can also favour La Niña-like conditions when the Seychelles–Chagos ridge warms [50]. Verdict: plausible amplifier of the late-1982 growth and peak, model-dependent (grade C).

Persistence and decay. In one coupled model, basin-wide Indian Ocean warming in DJF strengthens western-Pacific easterlies and hastens the El Niño–La Niña transition [51]. The observed 1983 sequence (basin warming, western-Pacific easterlies, then the weak 1983–84 La Niña) is consistent with that, but not tested for 1983 specifically (grade C).

What is not yet established

  • Westerly bursts. A complete 1982 episode catalogue has not yet been assembled in this review; Chu (1989), Sumathipala (1989) and Sadler (1983) remain to be retrieved, and the reanalysis burst list should be checked against JRA-55 or ERA5.
  • MJO, El Chichón and the Indian Ocean. Assessed above; all three contributions remain unresolved or model-dependent.
  • Full texts. Gill & Rasmusson (1983) could not be read at all; for the 1983 Science papers only abstracts were read (see the citation audit).
  • What was knowable at the time. The 1982 event was compared with a six-event composite [20] and departed markedly from the usual pattern [19] [2]; coupled-model forecasts began only with the 1986 event [3].
