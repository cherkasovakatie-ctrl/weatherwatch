Claim vs evidence

'95% chance the AMOC collapses': what the number means

Live Updated: 2026-10-30 · All claim-vs-evidence cards

Summary

No study we read says there is a 95 percent chance that the Atlantic overturning circulation (AMOC) collapses before 2100. The widely repeated '95%' is the width of an uncertainty interval around an estimated collapse year in one 2023 paper, which was corrected in 2025 (current text: central estimate 2065, interval 2037-2109). The IPCC has medium confidence that an abrupt collapse will not happen before 2100, while weakening is very likely. Direct measurements at 26 N show strong variability and a decline since about 2010; they are not a forecast.

What is known, forecast and unknown

Known: observation
  • Observed: RAPID 26 N array, 2004-2024, shows a decline since about 2010-11 and strong variability (rapid.ac.uk); the 2005-2023 mean transport is 16.9 Sv (as stated in Portmann et al. 2026).
  • Observed: North Atlantic sea-surface temperature records since 1870 (used indirectly in the 2023 paper).
Forecast: not an observation
  • Model-based, not observations: IPCC AR6 (weakening very likely; no abrupt collapse before 2100, medium confidence) [a4]; Baker et al. 2025 (34 models: weakening 20-81 percent, mean 54 percent, no collapse; collapse unlikely this century) [a6]; Portmann et al. 2026 (51 +/- 8 percent weakening by 2091-2100, SSP2-4.5) [a7]; Ditlevsen and Ditlevsen 2023 (statistical extrapolation: tipping year 2065, 95 percent interval 2037-2109) [a2]. Holden et al. 2026 preprint (shares of model runs in which collapse is locked in: 10 percent or 23 percent for a 2025 emissions peak; 82 to 100 percent under continued high emissions; mean delay 84 +/- 38 years) [a8]. These studies differ in method and in how close they put a tipping point.
Unknown
  • Whether sea-surface temperature tracks the AMOC well enough to date a collapse.
  • How close the real AMOC is to a tipping point (models disagree and may be biased toward stability).
  • Whether the committed-collapse numbers hold up: the preprint is not peer reviewed, uses one model, and its text is inconsistent on the collapse threshold and the number of experiments.

What the AMOC is

The AMOC is a system of Atlantic currents that carries warm water north near the surface and cold water south at depth. It is not the same thing as the Gulf Stream, which is wind-driven [a1].

Where '95%' comes from

Ditlevsen and Ditlevsen (2023) used North Atlantic sea-surface temperatures since 1870 as an indirect signal of the AMOC and extrapolated a trend toward a tipping point. In the current text the central estimate is 2065 with a 95 percent confidence interval of 2037-2109 [a2]. A 95 percent interval is a range for the year, not a 95 percent probability of collapse. The paper names no emissions scenario and assumes a steady drift; it says it cannot rule out other mechanisms [a2].

The 2025 correction

In August 2025 the authors corrected a code error that changed the tipping-time estimate by 8 years; figures, a table and estimates in the text were updated [a3]. The earlier coverage quoted about 2057, consistent with that shift, but the notice itself does not list the old and new values.

What other work says

The IPCC's sixth assessment has medium confidence that there will not be an abrupt collapse before 2100, and says weakening is very likely [a4]. A 2024 model study simulated a tipping event and argued the present AMOC is on a tipping course; its methods are debated [a5]. In 34 climate models forced by an abrupt quadrupling of CO2, the AMOC weakened by 20 to 81 percent (mean 54 percent) 90 years after the forcing but did not collapse; the authors conclude a twenty-first-century collapse (weakening below 6 Sv) is unlikely [a6].

A 2026 study (Portmann et al.) uses observations to constrain climate models and estimates a weakening of 51 +/- 8 percent (their 90 percent interval) by 2091-2100 relative to 1850-1900 under the SSP2-4.5 scenario, against 32 +/- 37 percent unconstrained. That is a weakening, not a collapse, and the authors caution that the interval rests on strong assumptions [a7]. A June 2026 preprint (Holden et al., not peer reviewed) uses 62 runs of one intermediate-complexity model and asks in how many a collapse is already locked in if emissions peak in 2025 and fall to zero over 35 years: 10 percent (23 percent with higher Greenland melt), and 82 to 100 percent under continued high emissions. These are shares of model runs, not forecasts; the collapse would come on average 84 +/- 38 years later, at the earliest about 2060. The text contradicts itself on its collapse threshold and number of experiments [a8].

What is observed

The RAPID array at 26 N has measured the overturning since 2004 and shows variability from days to a decade, with a sustained decline after 2010-11 [a9]. The proxy used for the 2023 estimate (sea-surface temperature) is questioned by other scientists [a10].

  2. a2✗ Verdict: unsupported as stated (an interval for a year, not a probability)

    95 percent chance the AMOC collapses before 2100

    Said by
    a video explainer; repeated in media
    What the source says
    Abstract: 'We estimate a collapse of the AMOC to occur around mid-century under the current scenario of future emissions'; the transition is 'most likely to occur around 2037-2109 (95% confidence interval)'. Maximum-likelihood method (Strang splitting): tipping year 2065, 95% interval 2037-2109. A second, moment-based method gives 2057. Table 1 (sea-surface-temperature proxy minus 1x, 2x or 3x the global mean): 2123 (2075-2288), 2065 (2037-2109), 2068 (2037-2101). The paper assumes its model is approximately correct, says 'we cannot rule out that other mechanisms are at play', does not exclude a partial collapse, and uses a sea-surface-temperature proxy. The 95 percent is the confidence interval of the estimated tipping year, not a 95 percent probability of collapse.

  6. a6✓ Verdict: confirmed (full text read by the PM)

    A complete AMOC collapse this century is unlikely

    Said by
    Baker et al., Nature 638, 987-994, published 26 Feb 2025 (corrected publication 2025)
    What the source says
    In 34 CMIP6 models forced by an abrupt quadrupling of CO2 held for 150 years, the AMOC weakened by 20 to 81 percent (mean 54 percent) 90 years after the forcing and 'does not collapse in the model experiments considered here'. In the seven models that also ran a 0.3 Sv freshwater experiment, the weakening was 50 to 80 percent in both experiments (means 56 and 61 percent). Southern Ocean wind-driven upwelling sustains the weakened AMOC; a Pacific overturning circulation emerges in almost all models but is too weak to balance it. Conclusion: 'a twenty-first century AMOC collapse (defined here as weakening to below 6 Sv) is unlikely'. The '90 years' are model years after the forcing in an idealised experiment, not calendar years.

  7. a7✓ Verdict: confirmed (PDF read by the PM)

    Observational constraints project about 50 percent AMOC weakening by the end of the century

    Said by
    Portmann et al., Science Advances 12, eadx4298, 15 April 2026
    What the source says
    Abstract: climate models suggest 32 +/- 37 percent weakening by 2100 (90 percent probability, SSP2-4.5, CMIP6); the best observational constraint (ridge-regularized regression on 19 observed variables) gives 51 +/- 8 percent, about 60 percent stronger than the multimodel mean. Table 1 (SSP2-4.5, 32 models): constrained 2091-2100 AMOC 8.1 +/- 1.4 Sv against a constrained 1850-1900 reference of 16.4 +/- 1.7 Sv; weakening 50.7 +/- 8.3 percent (unconstrained 32.0 +/- 36.7). Constrained, other scenarios: SSP1-2.6 19.5 +/- 25.4, SSP3-7.0 48.4 +/- 5.0, SSP5-8.5 57.7 +/- 5.4 percent. 'Weakening' is the 2091-2100 mean relative to 1850-1900. The authors say the 90 percent interval 'does not rigorously represent a probability interval' and 'should be treated with caution' (models treated as independent and equally plausible, observation error neglected). RAPID 2005-2023 mean: 16.9 Sv. An estimate of weakening, not a probability or a date of collapse.

  8. a8✓ Verdict: confirmed from the full text of an unreviewed preprint; internal inconsistencies noted

    AMOC collapse may already be locked in under the assumed emission path

    Said by
    Holden, Abrams, Bieger, Lenton, Mercure, Semieniuk and Sharpe, 'Quantifying the probability of committed AMOC collapse', EarthArXiv preprint v1, 27 June 2026, not peer reviewed
    What the source says
    One intermediate-complexity model (PLASIM-GENIE), 62 completed parameter sets of 69; runs of 300 years after a 1805-2005 historical run (to about 2305). 'Committed' means collapse happens even if emissions peak in a given year and then fall linearly to zero over 35 years. Collapse is defined as the AMOC falling below 45 percent of its preindustrial strength. For a 2025 peak: 10 percent of runs with baseline Greenland melt, 23 percent with higher Greenland melt, 3 percent with no extra melt; under continued high emissions (RCP8.5) to the end of the century: 82 percent, 100 percent and 61 percent. These are shares of model runs, not a forecast: the collapse itself comes on average 84 +/- 38 years after the commitment (earliest about 2060). The text contradicts itself on the collapse threshold (below 45 percent of preindustrial vs 45 percent below preindustrial) and on the number of experiments (21 or 23), so the numbers need confirmation by the authors or the peer-reviewed version.

Caveats

  • Verified by opening the source: the current text and corrected-publication PDF of Ditlevsen and Ditlevsen 2023, the Author Correction notice, rapid.ac.uk and the BODC EDMED page; read by the PM from user-supplied files: the full text of Baker et al. 2025, the PDF of Portmann et al. 2026 and the full text of the Holden et al. 2026 preprint. Not opened: the AR6 chapter, the van Westen paper and the Holden DOI link.
  • The collapse years are statistical estimates under stated assumptions, not predictions of a date; the Baker '90 years' are model years in an idealised experiment, not calendar years.
  • The Holden preprint is not peer reviewed. Its text defines collapse as the AMOC falling 'below 45% of preindustrial strength' in the Methods but as '45% below preindustrial' in Figure 2 and the text (45 or 55 percent remaining), and counts 21 experiments in the Introduction but 23 in the Methods. 'Committed' means collapse locked in under the assumed emission path, not that a collapse has happened or is certain.
  • This card describes statements, not the people who made them.
