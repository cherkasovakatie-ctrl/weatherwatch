Polar vortex, early November 2026: what is forecast and what would count

Live Updated: 2026-10-30 · All claim-vs-evidence cards

Summary

As of 30 October 2026, forecasts show the polar vortex above the Arctic weakening in early November, but not clearly reversing. Most forecasts do not show its winds fully reversing, and the official outlooks for 4-12 November favor above-normal temperatures across most of the United States, not a freeze. A confirmed 'sudden stratospheric warming' needs the winds to reverse, and any effect at the surface would take weeks. The tests below were fixed on 30 October, before the outcome.

What is known, forecast and unknown

Known: observation
  • Observed (analysis, approximate): 10 hPa 60N zonal-mean wind about +23 m/s on 27 Oct, about +15 to +17 m/s on 29-30 Oct (source: CPC chart).
Forecast: not an observation
  • Model forecast (GFS/GEFS, issued 2026-10-30): ensemble members fall toward about 0 to +5 m/s around 10-13 Nov; a few slightly negative [f2].
  • Human forecast (CPC, issued 2026-10-29 3 pm EDT, confidence 'about average, 3 out of 5' for 6-10 days): above-normal temperatures favored for most of the contiguous US 4-12 Nov [f3].
Unknown
  • Whether the wind reverses (no ECMWF forecast obtained; model counts quoted by news differ).
  • Whether any cold outbreak follows, and when: effects usually take weeks and vary from event to event.

What is the polar vortex?

The stratospheric polar vortex is a ring of strong west-to-east winds around the Arctic, roughly 10 to 50 km up. It normally strengthens through autumn. Forecasters follow it with the zonal-mean wind at 10 hPa and 60 degrees North [c1].

What is forecast (as issued on 30 October 2026)

The analysed wind rose to about +23 m/s around 27 October and fell to roughly +15 to +17 m/s on 29-30 October (read from a chart, approximate) [f1]. Forecast ensemble members fall toward about 0 to +5 m/s around 10-13 November; a few members dip slightly below zero [f2]. NOAA's Climate Prediction Center (29 October) favors above-normal temperatures for most of the contiguous United States on 4-12 November, with probabilities above 70 percent from the Mid-Atlantic to the Great Lakes for 4-8 November, and lists West Coast rain, snow and wind risks but no cold hazard [f3].

What would count as a sudden stratospheric warming

By the usual convention a major event requires the wind at 10 hPa and 60 N to turn easterly (below 0 m/s) [c2]. Such events occur about six times per decade in midwinter [c3]. A November event is rare; published counts differ [c4]. A weakening is not a major event.

What it may mean for weather

After weak-vortex events the surface often shows an Arctic Oscillation-like pattern over the following weeks, but not every time [c5]. The 2021 Texas freeze followed a January warming event; researchers disagree about whether one caused the other [c6].

What a popular video said

A video explainer from 26 October was careful that a freeze was not confirmed [c7], said the first week of November was not forecast to be freezing [c8], and said the European model flips the winds while the American model stops short [c9]. The table below rates each statement.

How to check

Tests fixed on: 2026-10-30 (before any outcome data)

  1. A (weakening): 10 hPa 60N zonal-mean zonal wind at or below +5 m/s on at least one day from 1 to 15 Nov 2026.
  2. B (major SSW, WMO type): the same wind below 0 m/s on at least one day from 1 to 30 Nov 2026.
  3. C (sustained reversal): below 0 m/s for at least 5 consecutive days, the first before 15 Nov 2026.
  4. D (surface response, evaluated only if B passes): daily or weekly mean Arctic Oscillation index negative on at least 7 of 14 days in a window starting 8 Nov to 8 Dec, and the NCEI CONUS monthly temperature anomaly for December 2026 below the 1991-2020 normal; each part reported separately.
