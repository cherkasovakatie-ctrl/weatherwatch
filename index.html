ONI: 2026 against the three strongest events

Oceanic Niño Index (ERSSTv6, 3-month running mean, centred base periods), aligned from DJF of each onset year. 2026 is the thick blue line; each analogue year has its own colour.

What makes 2026 different, and why

  • Fastest rise on record. From weak La Niña (−0.4 in DJF), like 1997, it reached +2.16 by JAS, higher than any event at this stage (1997 +1.79, 2015 +1.73). Why: the trade winds collapsed (−2 to −3 m/s anomalies) and released the warm water stored in the west.
  • Record fuel below the surface. Upper-300 m heat content reached +3.39 °C in September, far above 1997's +2.56. Why it matters: subsurface heat leads the surface by 1–3 months, so more warming is still in the pipeline.
  • Extremely east-based. Niño 1+2 off Peru hit +4.7 °C (monthly), a record — stronger even than 1997 (+3.7), and nothing like centre-heavy 2015 (+2.5). East-based events disrupt the Pacific jet stream most, the classic recipe for a wet California winter.
  • A warmer world underneath. The relative index (RONI) is +1.69, about 0.5 °C below the ONI: the whole tropical ocean is unusually warm, so part of the ONI is background warming, not El Niño. In 1997 the two were almost equal.
  • A hostile North Pacific. The PDO is −1.55; in 1982, 1997 and 2015 it was positive. A negative PDO can blunt El Niño's effect on North America — the main wild card for this winter.

The wild card: a strong El Niño meets a negative PDO

What the PDO is. The Pacific Decadal Oscillation describes the North Pacific's slow temperature pattern. In its positive phase the water along North America is warm and the central North Pacific cool; in its negative phase the reverse. It shifts over years to decades, while ENSO shifts over months. In September 2026 the PDO was −1.55, strongly negative. Among El Niño winters since 1951, only 2023–24 had a lower PDO. In 1982, 1997 and 2015 it was positive (+0.5, +1.8, +1.0).

Why it could matter. El Niño's heat in the tropics strengthens the subtropical jet stream and deepens the Aleutian Low in the Gulf of Alaska. That steers storms into California. A positive PDO works in the same direction. A negative PDO pushes the other way: it favours high pressure over the northeast Pacific, the blocking "ridge" that diverts storms north to Canada and leaves California dry. So the two patterns are pulling in opposite directions this winter.

What history actually shows. The record is more reassuring than the theory. In Downtown Los Angeles, all four El Niño winters with a negative PDO were wetter than normal, including 2023–24 (PDO −1.7, rain 156% of normal). The two driest strong El Niño winters, 2015–16 (48%) and 1986–87 (40%), both had a positive PDO. The PDO sign alone has not decided Los Angeles rainfall.

Why it is still the wild card. No winter on record combines an El Niño this strong (ONI above +2) with a PDO this negative, so there is no true analogue. Ten winters are too few to rule anything out. And the September PNA was −0.64, the opposite of the usual El Niño pattern. A North Pacific ridge has not yet given way to the deep Aleutian Low that brings storms south.

What to watch, November–January

  • PDO trend (NOAA NCEI, monthly). A climb toward zero would remove most of the conflict.
  • PNA turning positive (CPC). That would mean the Aleutian Low is deepening and the storm track is shifting south.
  • Ridge watch on this dashboard. A persistent ridge near 130°W is the dry scenario.
  • Atmospheric-river forecasts. Big El Niño winters deliver most of their rain in a few strong AR storms.

Verdict

  • Real overlap off Southern California. El Niño warmth arriving on top of a local marine heatwave is the 2015 sequence in miniature; NOAA says the local warmth may turn into El Niño-driven warming in the coming months, as it did in 2015.
  • Blob-like in persistence, not in size. The California heatwave is 17 months old and NOAA expects it to last through 2026, but there is no basin-scale warm pool, no persistent ridge, and the PDO is strongly negative.
  • A 2025 caveat. The giant North Pacific heatwave widely reported in 2025 was last year's event; NOAA ranked it the fourth-largest Northeast Pacific heatwave on record.

What would change the verdict

  • NOAA's tracker reporting the two heatwaves joining or reaching the coast north of Point Conception.
  • A ridge holding off the West Coast for weeks (ridge watch).
  • The PDO climbing toward zero or turning positive.

Why the climb steepened after the mid-1970s: our best reading

  • Greenhouse gases are the main driver. CO₂ at Mauna Loa rose from about 332 ppm in 1976 to 426 ppm in 2026, and methane and nitrous oxide climbed too. The extra heat they trap grows with every year of emissions. The IPCC (2021) attributes essentially all warming since the late 1800s to human activity.
  • The cooling haze lifted. From the 1940s to the 1970s, sulphate pollution from coal and oil reflected sunlight and largely masked greenhouse warming; that is the flat stretch in the middle of the chart. Clean-air laws in North America and Europe from the 1970s, and cleaner ship fuel from 2020, removed much of that mask.
  • The Pacific flipped. In 1976–77 the Pacific Decadal Oscillation switched to its warm phase for about two decades. That set the timing of the turn, while greenhouse gases set the long-term direction.
  • Natural ups and downs ride on top. Strong El Niños make spikes (1998, 2016, 2023–24); large volcanic eruptions cause dips (El Chichón 1982, Pinatubo 1991–93). The Sun is not the cause: its output has been flat to slightly lower since the 1980s while temperatures rose.
  • 2023–2026. The recent jump of about 0.3 °C combines the long-term trend, two strong El Niños, less ship pollution and fewer low clouds reflecting sunlight. Scientists are still debating how much each contributed.

Carbon dioxide at Mauna Loa since 1958

Monthly mean (seasonal saw-tooth) and the de-seasonalised trend, ppm, NOAA GML.

What the curve tells us

  • It keeps speeding up. CO₂ rose about 0.8 ppm a year in the 1960s, 1.5 in the 1980s, 2.0 in the 2000s and about 2.7 a year over 2015–2025. Burning coal, oil and gas releases more than land and ocean can absorb; roughly half of each year's emissions stays in the air.
  • The saw-tooth is the planet breathing. Each year CO₂ peaks in May and falls to a low in September–October, as Northern Hemisphere forests and crops take up carbon in summer and release it in winter (430.5 ppm in May 2025, 424.4 in September).
  • El Niño gives it a kick. Growth jumps in El Niño years (1998, 2016, 2024) because droughts and fires in the tropics weaken plant uptake, usually 6–12 months after the El Niño peaks. With the record 2026 event, faster growth is likely in 2027. A single month's year-on-year change (+1.7 ppm, Sep 2026) is noisy, so look at the trend line.
  • Why it matters for temperature. CO₂ lasts centuries in the air, so the warming it causes builds up; the 1880–2026 temperature chart above follows this curve, with natural wiggles on top.

Northern Hemisphere snow cover anomaly

Monthly land snow cover minus the 1991–2020 average for that month, million km², Rutgers Global Snow Lab, since 2015.

What the snow record tells us

  • Spring and early-summer snow is disappearing fastest. Since 1972, June snow cover has shrunk by about 1.3 million km² per decade (from a June average of about 9 million km²), and March by about 0.5 million km² per decade. In 2026, February (−2.4), March (−2.0) and June (−1.9 million km²) were all well below normal.
  • Why that matters. Snow reflects most sunlight; bare ground absorbs it. Earlier melt warms the ground and air, which melts more snow, one of the feedbacks that makes the Arctic warm about four times faster than the global average. It also shifts river flow earlier, leaving less water in late summer.
  • Autumn is different. October and December show slight increases in this record (about +0.6 and +0.3 million km² per decade). Part may be real, since warmer air carries more moisture into early-winter Siberia, and part may reflect changes in how satellites mapped snow in the early years.
  • Now. September 2026 was 0.5 million km² below normal, so the new season is starting slightly behind.

How to read these panels

Temperature, CO₂ and methane rise and fall together through each glacial cycle; sea level follows with the growth and melt of ice sheets. Ice-core temperature is local to central Antarctica, where glacial–interglacial swings are roughly twice the global mean. The ice-core gases are direct measurements of ancient air; the temperature and sea-level curves are reconstructions. Sea level carries a 1σ uncertainty of 9–12 m according to its authors, so the stack's value of about +9 m at 0 ka is within error of zero rather than a real offset. The cave record mainly tracks the strength of the summer monsoon and Northern Hemisphere summer sunshine (the 23,000-year precession cycle), so its rhythm is faster than the ice-age cycle. None of these panels includes post-industrial measurements; those are under Modern context.

How to read this chart

Benthic δ18O mixes two signals: how much water is locked in land ice, and how cold the deep ocean is. Both move the same way, so higher values mean a colder, icier world. The long drift toward higher values is the cooling from the warm Pliocene into the ice ages. The cycles grow larger and longer: about 41,000 years before roughly 1.2 million years ago, then about 100,000 years after the Mid-Pleistocene Transition (MPT, shaded, about 1.2–0.8 Ma). The age model is tuned to orbital cycles, so its timing is not independent of them.

The same measurement as the 5.3-million-year view, extended across the whole Cenozoic by splicing deep-sea cores. Before about 34 million years ago there were no large ice sheets, so δ18O mainly reflects deep-ocean temperature; after that it also records ice volume. Tooth enamel of humans and other mammals, from museum collections, cave deposits and archaeological sites.
What is measured?
Oxygen isotope ratios (δ18O), and often carbon isotopes (δ13C), sampled along the enamel's daily growth lines by ion microprobe, laser or micro-drill.
What can it reconstruct?
The isotopic signature of the water an animal drank and ate, which tracks rainfall seasonality, monsoon strength and dry spells; carbon isotopes indicate diet and vegetation (forest versus grassland).
Period and resolution
The few years in which the tooth formed, at weekly to monthly resolution. Fossil teeth are dated separately, for example by uranium series or radiocarbon on associated material.
What can distort it?
Nursing (milk is isotopically enriched), drinking behaviour and diet, altitude, post-burial alteration of fossils, and the unknown isotope value of local rain. Enamel isotopes are not a thermometer and do not give rainfall amounts.

Example: Smith et al. 2024, orangutan teeth.

Natural archive

Tree rings

Growing-season weather → ring width, wood density, isotopes → cross-dated to the exact year → calibrated against instrumental records → inferred temperature or drought

What material?
Living trees, historic timbers and preserved (subfossil) wood.
What is measured?
Ring width, maximum latewood density and stable isotopes in each annual ring.
What can it reconstruct?
Summer temperature where cold limits growth (treelines, high latitudes) and drought, rainfall or streamflow where water limits growth.
Period and resolution
Annual, sometimes seasonal; cross-dating ring patterns between trees gives exact calendar years, and long chronologies stitch living and dead wood back thousands of years.
What can distort it?
Tree age, competition, disturbance and rising CO₂ also change growth; removing age trends can remove slow climate trends; some northern sites show weaker temperature response since the mid-20th century (the "divergence problem"); coverage is mostly land in the mid and high latitudes.

Archive: NOAA's International Tree-Ring Data Bank, more than 5,000 sites on six continents.

Natural archive

Ice cores

Snowfall and air → buried and compressed into layered ice with trapped bubbles → isotopes, gases, dust and sulphate measured → inferred local temperature, greenhouse gases, volcanism

What material?
Cores drilled through the Antarctic and Greenland ice sheets and mountain glaciers.
What is measured?
Water isotopes (δD, δ18O) in the ice; CO₂, methane and nitrous oxide in trapped air; dust, sea salt, volcanic sulphate and ash.
What can it reconstruct?
Local temperature when the snow fell (from isotopes); past greenhouse-gas levels directly, since the bubbles are samples of old air; dustiness, wind and volcanic eruptions.
Period and resolution
Up to about 800,000 years in Antarctica (EPICA Dome C); annual layers can be counted near the top, while deep ice is dated with flow models and tie points and averages centuries to millennia per sample.
What can distort it?
The isotope–temperature relationship shifts with moisture source and the season of snowfall; ice-sheet height changes; layers thin with depth; air in bubbles is younger than the surrounding ice; one site is not a global average.

Example: Jouzel et al. 2007, EPICA Dome C.

Sources and uncertainty

Method notes

  • Published reconstructions only. This tab plots reconstructions as published. It does not convert raw proxy measurements into temperature or rainfall itself.
  • Dating conventions. "BP" means years before 1950 CE. "ka" means thousand years before present. Radiocarbon ages are calibrated in the source studies; uranium-series ages on fossils are often minimum ages.
  • Reference periods differ. Temperature 12k uses 1800–1900 CE; PAGES 2k uses 1961–1990; GISTEMP uses 1951–1980; the IPCC uses 1850–1900; EPICA uses the last 1,000 years; sea level is relative to present. Never compare numbers across these without re-baselining.
  • δ18O is not temperature. Cave and deep-sea δ18O are measured values. Cave δ18O mainly tracks monsoon strength and moisture source; benthic δ18O mixes deep-ocean temperature and ice volume. They are shown as measured, with inverted axes so up means wetter or warmer.
  • Time windows are not spliced. Each window uses different archives and age models (EDC3, AICC2012, LR04, U–Th, astronomical tuning). The ice-core gas panels stop at 1750 CE; the industrial era is shown only under Modern context.
  • Resolution limits comparison. A 100-year bin cannot show a single hot decade. The most recent instrumental decade (2015–2024) was 1.24 °C above 1850–1900 (Forster et al. 2025), but that is a 10-year average and cannot be read directly against centennial bins.
  • Coverage gaps. Proxy records cluster on land in the Northern Hemisphere; oceans, the tropics and the Southern Hemisphere are thinner, which widens uncertainty there.

Method and caveats

  • Earthquakes: USGS ComCat, all M6+ events since 1950. Aftershock sequences inflate yearly counts, so the main tests use mainshocks only, after Gardner–Knopoff declustering.
  • Eruptions: Smithsonian GVP Volcanoes of the World 5.4, confirmed eruptions. Reporting has improved over time, so counts are tested after removing a 21-year running mean.
  • Cyclone energy: ACE from NHC HURDAT2 (Atlantic, eastern and central Pacific, through 2025) and JTWC tracks in IBTrACS (other basins, final data through November 2024). The 2026 season is not in the best-track archives yet.
  • Correlation is not causation. The cyclone links are physically understood: El Niño raises vertical wind shear over the Atlantic and shifts warm water and typhoon genesis east in the Pacific. No accepted physical mechanism links ENSO to earthquakes.
  • The volcano test is limited: only four VEI 5+ tropical eruptions occurred in the period, and El Chichón (1982) and Pinatubo (1991) erupted as El Niño events were already developing.

Earth's magnetic field: a growing weak spot and two deep "blobs"

The South Atlantic Anomaly is growing. Over the South Atlantic, southeast of South America, the magnetic field is weaker than anywhere else. Eleven years of ESA Swarm satellite data show the weak area has grown since 2014 by roughly half the size of continental Europe, and southwest of Africa the field has weakened even faster since 2020. Over the same period the field strengthened over Siberia and weakened over Canada. The cause lies at the top of Earth's liquid core: "reverse flux patches" where the field points back into the core instead of out of it.

Why it matters. A weaker field lets more radiation reach satellites as they pass over the region, which can cause glitches, hardware damage and blackouts. ESA mentions no effect on weather or climate.

The deep "blobs". About 3,000 km down, just above the core, two continent-sized masses of hot rock sit beneath Africa and the Pacific (geologists call them large low-shear-velocity provinces, because seismic waves slow down inside them). Biggin et al. (2026) found they are hotter than the surrounding mantle and draw less heat out of the core, which has shaped the magnetic field for at least the last few hundred million years. They move millimetres per year: nothing about them changes on a human timescale, and they have no link to the ocean "Blob" or El Niño.

Two timescales

  • Years to decades: the South Atlantic Anomaly and the shifting strong-field regions, driven by flow in the liquid outer core. Measured directly by satellites.
  • Millions of years: the deep blobs, which set the background against which the core's flow works. Read from the magnetism locked into rocks up to about 250 million years old.
  • Not connected to the weather. Ocean heat, El Niño and marine heatwaves are driven by winds and the upper few hundred metres of the sea.

Sources: ESA, "Swarm reveals growing weak spot in Earth's magnetic field" (13 Oct 2025); Biggin, A. J. et al., "Mantle heterogeneity influenced Earth's ancient magnetic field", Nature Geoscience 19, 345–352 (2026), published online 3 Feb 2026; University of Liverpool summary (4 Feb 2026).

How this was tested

Predictors (monthly, 15th of each month): closeness (cosine of separation) and hard aspects (within 8° of 0°, 90° or 180°) for ten outer-planet pairs, heliocentric clustering of the planets, the number of retrograde planets, and the lunar node cycle. Indices: ONI, PDO, AMO, NAO, PNA, AO, AAO and global temperature with its long-term trend removed. Slow cycles and persistent climate indices produce chance correlations easily, so each p-value comes from surrogate indices with the same persistence, and the whole set is corrected for multiple testing. Forward test: these four leads are now fixed in advance and will be re-scored as new months arrive. Physical note: the planets' tidal pull on Earth is about a million times smaller than the Moon's, so a real effect would need an indirect route, for example through the Sun.

How to read the figures

  • Weekly OISST is the closest to the live ocean here. Monthly OISST trails it by a few weeks, and ONI and RONI, as 3-month means centred on the middle month, trail it by about two months.
  • Relative indices (RONI, relative OISST) subtract the tropical-mean anomaly. They show how unusual the Niño regions are against the rest of the tropics, which matters when the whole tropical ocean is warm.
  • CPC's SOI is in standard deviations. Dashboards that quote a −7 threshold use the BOM scale, about ten times larger.
  • Analogue charts compare shapes, not outcomes. Each strong event had its own winter impacts.
