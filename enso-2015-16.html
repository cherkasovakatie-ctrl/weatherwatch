Abstract

The 2015–16 El Niño peaked at +2.25 on NOAA's Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI) in NDJ 2015–16 and at +2.59 on the traditional ONI: on the relative index it is the weakest of the three super events in this series, on the traditional index the strongest, and within observational uncertainty the three cannot be separated [3] [14]. Its development was a two-year story. A recharged Pacific and strong westerly bursts in early 2014 set up a widely forecast major event that stalled: westerly bursts stopped from April to June, a basin-wide easterly burst in June knocked the Niño indices back to normal, and a cold south-eastern Pacific diverted the winds [16] [17] [20]. The same easterly burst stopped the warm water from draining away, leaving the basin charged for 2015 [18]. From March 2015 a strong burst, then an unusually long run of bursts through the year — the most in any year of the 1979–2016 reanalysis — drove four downwelling Kelvin waves and coupled growth to a peak in November–January [13] [6]. The event was centred further west than 1982–83 and 1997–98, with record Niño 4 and weaker warming at the South American coast, consistent with an added role for central-Pacific, subtropically forced dynamics; a North Pacific coupled mode linked to the 2013–15 'Blob' is a proposed Northern Hemisphere trigger [12] [15] [27]. TAO mooring reporting declined sharply in 2012–14, so the failed 2014 onset was observed with far fewer moorings than 1997 [35] [5].

Main findings

  1. Precondition (grade A−; high confidence). Warm water volume stayed positive from mid-2013; the June 2014 easterly burst prevented and then reversed the discharge that a 2014 El Niño would have caused, giving 2015 a head start [3] [18].
  2. A failed trigger in 2014 (grade A; high confidence). Two strong bursts in January–March 2014, then none from April to June; a wind-forced ocean model reproduces both the 2014 stall and the 2015 growth from bursts and easterly surges alone [16] [19] [6].
  3. Suppressors in 2014 (grade A for the easterly burst, B/C for the meridional gradient). A basin-wide easterly burst in June and a south-eastern Pacific cold anomaly that drove meridional rather than zonal winds [17] [20].
  4. Trigger and growth in 2015 (grade A−; high confidence). A strong burst in March 2015 and a record number of bursts through the year drove four downwelling Kelvin waves from April to November [6] [13] [5].
  5. Pattern (grade B; medium–high confidence). Westward-centred warming, record Niño 4 and a weak coastal thermocline anomaly: eastern-Pacific dynamics plus stronger central-Pacific dynamics [13] [15] [12].
  6. North Pacific link (grade B/C; medium–low confidence). A Victoria-mode/meridional-mode pathway from the North Pacific is proposed for this event; it rests on one statistical study and general hindcasts [27] [23].
  7. Background warming (grade C). The index adjustment lowers the peak by 0.34 °C, the most of the three events; one review attributes the extremity partly to background warming, but no event attribution is made here [2] [11].

1. Introduction

Early in 2014 the tropical Pacific looked much as it had in early 1997: a recharged equatorial heat content, an eastward-displaced warm pool and strong westerly bursts. Many models forecast a strong El Niño, some an extreme one [16] [17]. It did not happen in 2014; it happened, at extreme strength, in 2015. That makes 2015–16 the only one of the three historical super events with a documented false start, and a natural test of what decides whether a charged Pacific turns into a major El Niño. The forecasts for 2015 itself were good, in contrast to the 'overexuberant' predictions of the year before [12].

This paper reconstructs the sequence from mid-2013 to the La Niña of late 2016 with the same framework, evidence grades and primary index (RONI) as Papers 1 and 2, and with identical reanalysis diagnostics, so that the three events can be compared.

2. Definitions, data and methods

Index. RONI is the primary index [1] [2]; ONI is shown alongside. The choice matters more for this event than for the others: removing the tropical-mean warming lowers the peak from +2.59 to +2.25.

Observations. Argo floats and satellite altimetry, winds and salinity were all available. The TAO/TRITON array was not: its reporting declined dramatically from mid-2012 to early 2014 [35]. In the moorings used here, daily equatorial wind records exist for 33–41% of site-days from July 2013 to March 2014 and 36% in April–June 2014, against 83–97% from October 2014 to September 2015 [5].

Reanalysis diagnostics. The burst and MJO diagnostics of Papers 1 and 2 were re-run for July 2013 – December 2016 with the same definitions: 1000 hPa anomaly of at least 5 m s⁻¹ for at least 3 days over at least 10° of longitude, 5°S–5°N, 120°E–150°W, against a 1979–2000 climatology, with the MJO index patterns fitted to 1979–2001 [6]. Because easterly bursts are broad rather than intense, they are described with the 150°E–100°W mean anomaly instead of the burst rule. Kelvin waves are traced in TAO 20 °C isotherm depth where the moorings reported.

Evidence grades are those of Papers 1 and 2 (A: event observation plus a model or budget study isolating the effect; B: event observation with a plausible mechanism; C: correlation, composite or theory; D: speculative or untested). Several key studies were read only as abstracts; the reference list records what was read.

3. Pre-event conditions and the false start of 2014

A charged Pacific. After weak cool conditions in 2013 (RONI −0.48 in JJA 2013), warm water volume was positive throughout July 2013 – February 2014 (+0.78 to +1.25 × 10¹⁴ m³) [3] [7]. In winter 2013–14 an exceptional warm anomaly — the 'Blob' — formed in the north-eastern Pacific, the largest there since at least the 1980s, through reduced ocean heat loss and weak cold advection under persistently high pressure [25].

Early 2014: the onset that stalled. The reanalysis shows two strong westerly bursts, on 24 Jan 2014 – 29 Jan 2014 (+9.5 m s⁻¹ at 145°E) and 19 Feb 2014 – 5 Mar 2014 (+9.2 m s⁻¹ at 155°E), and then none until a weak one in August; 2014 has 3 bursts in all [6]. Heat content east of the Date Line rose from −0.33 °C in January to +1.60 °C in March and warm water volume peaked at +2.05 × 10¹⁴ m³ [3]. Until April the warm pool was displaced east as at the onset of strong events, but by July it had retreated: in ocean simulations the difference from 1997 is the absence of westerly events from April to June [16]. A wind-forced model built from the available buoy winds explains the early development and mid-year reversal by a springtime surplus of westerly events followed by a summer balance between westerly events and easterly surges [19].

Suppressors. In June an unusually strong, basin-wide easterly burst preceded a rapid fall of all major Niño indices to near normal; afterwards two separate warm centres persisted in the central and eastern Pacific and the Bjerknes feedback did not engage [17]. In the reanalysis this easterly burst is broad rather than intense: the 150°E–100°W mean 1000 hPa anomaly reaches -1.94 m s⁻¹ on 11 Jun 2014, the 5th-lowest of the 1280 days analysed, yet no 5 m s⁻¹ easterly burst meets the burst rule [6]. A south-eastern Pacific cold anomaly with warmth to the north drove strong meridional winds near the equator rather than the zonal winds that growth needs; in forecast experiments it accounts for about 40% of the 2014 amplitude error [20].

The head start. The same easterly burst prevented, and then reversed, the discharge of equatorial heat content that a 2014 El Niño would have caused; warm water volume rebuilt to +1.49 and +1.57 × 10¹⁴ m³ in August–September 2014 [18] [3]. Weak warm conditions persisted (RONI +0.62 in NDJ 2014–15; ONI ≥ +0.5 from SON 2014), but by conventional definitions the 2014 event failed to qualify as an El Niño [18].

4. Development, March – December 2015

Trigger. The decisive burst came on 4 Mar 2015 – 16 Mar 2015: +12.4 m s⁻¹ at 167.5°E, spanning 140°–175°E, during an active MJO (OMI 2.0) [6]. The restored TAO array traced its downwelling Kelvin wave from 165°E on 11 Mar 2015 to 110°W on 12 Apr 2015, 32 days at about 3.4 m s⁻¹; later waves left 165°E on 11 May 2015 and 4 Jul 2015 [5]. From April to November four downwelling Kelvin waves initiated and enhanced the eastern-central warming, and eastward current anomalies advected the warm pool east in spring and summer [13]. A freshwater anomaly in the equatorial Pacific larger than anything in the 2004–2014 Argo record raised steric height in the west and increased eastward surface acceleration at the salinity front [32].

Coupling. RONI rose from +0.89 (AMJ) to +1.27 (JJA), with SOI −0.6 to −1.1 and Niño 1+2 reaching +2.48 °C in July; public El Niño advisories were issued in the first half of 2015 [3] [12]. Unlike 1997, when most bursts preceded coupling, bursts kept coming as the event grew: 12 from late June to October, several centred east of the Date Line, and 17 in calendar 2015 — the most of any year in 1979–2016 (1997 had 16, 1982 had 13; the 1979–2001 mean is 4.2) [6]. A wind-forced ocean model attributes the rapid 2015 warming to this abundance of westerly events [19].

Peak. RONI reached +2.03 (SON), +2.18 (OND) and +2.25 (NDJ); central-Pacific OLR fell to −49.9 W m⁻² in December [3]. Niño 4 set a record and Niño 3 matched the earlier super events, but Niño 1+2 was much weaker; enhanced convection lay about 20° further west, and the maximum thermocline anomaly near South America was only a third to a half of that in 1982 and 1997 [13]. Unlike previous major El Niños, SST anomalies were remarkably high in the western and central equatorial Pacific and milder near the coast of South America [12]. The 1997–98 event followed eastern-Pacific dynamics based on basin-wide thermocline changes; 2015–16 added central-Pacific dynamics dependent on subtropical forcing [15] [34].

5. Termination and transition, November 2015 – 2016

An upwelling Kelvin wave emerged in early November 2015 and led to a rapid decline [13]; warm water volume turned negative in December (−0.39 × 10¹⁴ m³) and fell to −3.36 × 10¹⁴ m³ by April 2016 [3]. RONI fell from +1.81 (JFM) to +0.08 (AMJ) and the event ended in May 2016 [3] [12]. During decay the SST anomalies lingered around the Date Line instead of retreating to the South American coast as in 1997–98 [15]. The southward shift of westerly anomalies in boreal winter, tied to the seasonal strengthening of the South Pacific Convergence Zone, is a key element in the termination of strong El Niño events in general [31]; its role in 2016 was not isolated in the studies read here. A weak La Niña followed (RONI −0.96 in SON 2016), promoted by a strong negative Indian Ocean Dipole that developed mainly from Indian Ocean conditions [28].

6. Interactions with the broader climate system

Intraseasonal variability. The MJO was active (OLR MJO index ≥ 1) on 3 Mar 2015 – 2 Apr 2015 and 29 May 2015 – 22 Jul 2015 (maximum 2.89), framing the March and late-June bursts [6]. Intraseasonal OLR variance at 160°E–150°W in October 2015 – March 2016 was 303 W² m⁻⁴, nearly twice that of October 1996 – March 1997 (166), as convection shifted east with the warm water [6]. In a model, El Niño-like SST shifts the MJO onto the equator and strengthens it in the central Pacific, favouring bursts [22]; a comparison of the three super events finds 2015–16 alone kept a prominent eastward-propagating MJO in its mature stage [10]. MJO events warm the waveguide only when they contain westerly bursts [8]. Every El Niño of 1971–2010 was accompanied by clustered westerly bursts, and whether they produce a central- or eastern-Pacific event depends on their timing within the recharge–discharge cycle [21]; in 2015 they fell on a basin already recharged by the aborted 2014 event.

North Pacific. The Blob persisted for several years and the PDO was positive from 2014 (up to +1.86 in December 2014) [26] [3]. One analysis identifies the Blob as part of an enhanced Victoria-mode coupled pattern forced by a North Pacific Oscillation-like circulation from 2013, a precursor of ENSO that bridged to the 2015–16 El Niño, which it describes as triggered from the Northern Hemisphere, unlike other large events [27]. For the earlier super events a Southern Hemisphere circulation 'booster' had been identified [24]. Hindcast experiments find that the North Pacific Oscillation-induced meridional mode influences ENSO evolution more lastingly than a March MJO [23]. The evidence for 2015 itself remains statistical (grade B/C).

South Pacific. The 2014 south-eastern Pacific cold anomaly acted as a suppressor (above) [20].

Indian Ocean. The dipole index was weakly positive in August–October 2015 (CPC DMI +0.42 to +0.47 °C) [3]; IOD phase is largely set by ENSO through combination-mode wind and heat-flux forcing [29]. The strong negative IOD of 2016 mattered for the transition: without it the 2016 La Niña may have been substantially weaker [28].

Volcanic forcing. The tropical eruptions of VEI 4 in the period were Sinabung (September 2013, 3°N), Kelud (February 2014, 8°S) and Wolf (May 2015, on the equator); none was larger [4]. No study read here links them to the 2014–16 evolution (not a supported candidate).

Background warming. The tropical-mean adjustment lowers the peak by 0.34 °C (ONI +2.59, RONI +2.25), the largest difference of the three events [3] [2]. A review attributes the extremity of the event in part to the unusually warm 2014 conditions and to long-term background warming [11]; the rapid global temperature rise of 2014–16 has been explained by heat release from these El Niño conditions on top of the warming trend [33]. With SST uncertainty the three strongest events are not separable [14]. No event-specific attribution of amplitude is made here.

7. Causal synthesis and competing explanations

The most plausible sequence is: (1) the equatorial Pacific was recharged from 2013; (2) early-2014 bursts started an onset that stalled without spring bursts, under a June easterly burst and a cold south-eastern Pacific; (3) the easterly burst kept the heat in the basin; (4) a strong burst in March 2015 and a record run of bursts through 2015 drove repeated Kelvin waves and coupled growth; (5) central-Pacific dynamics and a North Pacific precursor gave the event its westward-shifted pattern; (6) an upwelling Kelvin wave from November 2015 and discharge ended it by May 2016.

Competing views concern weights rather than ingredients: whether the 2014 stall was mainly the missing spring bursts [16], the easterly burst [17], a balance of both [19] or the meridional SST gradient [20]; and whether the North Pacific precursor was a cause or a parallel response to the same atmospheric forcing [27]. Many 2015 bursts came after coupling had begun and are probably partly a response to it [9] [22].

8. What was forecast at the time

The predictions made in 2014 were 'overexuberant': models anticipated a strong event that did not develop [12]. A climate model with superimposed wind bursts suggests inherent limits to predictability once an easterly burst of this kind occurs [17]. In 2015 the life cycle and magnitude were well predicted by most models used by national meteorological services [12].

9. Uncertainties

  • TAO data return in 2014 was poor, so wind-forced reconstructions of the 2014 stall depend on gap-filled winds [19] [5].
  • The burst count uses a 1979–2000 climatology; any trend in the background winds since then affects counts in 2014–16.
  • Several key studies (Xue and Kumar; Tseng et al.; Gasparin and Roemmich; Lim and Hendon) were read only as abstracts.
  • The North Pacific link rests mainly on one statistical analysis.

10. Conclusion

The 2015–16 El Niño was built over two years. The ocean was ready in 2014, but the winds were not: without spring bursts, and with a strong June easterly burst and a cold south-eastern Pacific, the 2014 onset stalled. That failure kept the heat in place, and in 2015 a sustained, record run of westerly bursts turned it into an extreme, westward-shifted El Niño with a weaker coastal signature than 1982–83 or 1997–98. Compared with those events the trigger is again the westerly bursts, the precondition again the recharge — and what differs is the path from one to the other.

Evidence base: scope, index and data

Primary index: NOAA's Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI) [1] [2]. On RONI the 2015–16 peak is +2.25 (NDJ 2015), the weakest of the three historical events in this series (1982–83 +2.40, 1997–98 +2.28); on the traditional ONI it is +2.59, the strongest. The difference (0.34 °C) is the tropical-mean warming that RONI removes. Within SST uncertainty the three events cannot be ranked [14].

Observing system. Argo floats (from the 2000s) and satellite altimetry, salinity and winds were available, but TAO mooring data return had declined sharply from mid-2012 to early 2014, so the 2014 onset attempt is less well observed at the equator than 1997 [35] [19] [36].
