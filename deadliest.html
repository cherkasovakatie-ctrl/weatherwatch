How to read these numbers

  • Death tolls measure vulnerability as much as weather. The deadliest storms struck crowded, low-lying deltas and flood plains at night, with little warning and no shelter. Comparable storms today kill far fewer: Bangladesh lost 300,000 or more in 1970, about 139,000 in 1991, and far fewer in later storms of similar strength after building shelters and a volunteer warning system.
  • Many deaths come after the event. In the 1931 China floods, the 1876 Bengal cyclone and the 1975 Banqiao disaster, famine and epidemics killed as many people as the water, or more. Whether a figure includes them is the main reason estimates differ.
  • Old numbers are often not counts. Before the 20th century, and in places without death registration, tolls are chronicle figures, round numbers or later compilations. Twelve of the twenty headline figures here are rated low confidence; for three (Coringa 1839, North Sea 1099, Chittagong 1897), the commonly quoted figure could not be traced to any contemporary count.
  • Heat-wave tolls are statistical. They are estimated as deaths above the expected number, or modelled from the temperature–mortality relationship, so they are well measured but not directly comparable with counted drownings.
  • Earthquakes, tsunamis, volcanoes and famines with non-weather causes are excluded. Droughts that led to famine are also left out here, because their tolls depend mainly on politics and food systems.

Confidence ratings are this site's judgement: high means a systematic count or epidemiological estimate; medium a contemporary official or survey estimate with known gaps; low a compilation figure without a traceable contemporary count. Each event page lists its sources. Prepared 17 October 2026.
